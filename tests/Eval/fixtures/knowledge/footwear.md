## Boots

All Pinewood boots are made on our own last, a foot shape that is a little wider at the front than most brands. If other boots press on your little toes, ours may fit better. Each model below has the same table, so you can compare them line by line.

The most important line is the sole stiffness. We give every boot a grade from 1 to 4:

- **Grade 1**: soft and bendy. Good for paths and gravel tracks, and the most comfortable on the first day.
- **Grade 2**: bends at the ball of the foot, but the middle is firm. Good for most hill walks.
- **Grade 3**: stiff. Your feet do not tire on rocks and on steep, rough ground with a heavy pack.
- **Grade 4**: almost rigid, with a strong edge. For snow, ice and the coldest days.

A stiffer boot gives more support, but it feels heavier and takes longer to break in. Most people who walk on marked paths in summer are happy with grade 1 or 2.

The waterproof membrane in our boots is called PineDry. It is a thin layer between the outer material and the lining that keeps water out and lets sweat out as steam. A membrane makes a boot warmer, so in hot weather a boot without one can be the better choice. When water comes in over the top of the boot, a membrane also keeps it in, so for walks through deep streams, wear gaiters.

### Finding the right fit

A good boot holds your heel firmly and gives your toes room to move. This is how our shop staff check the fit, and you can do the same at home.

1. Put on the socks you will walk in. Before you lace up, push your foot forward until your toes touch the front. One finger should fit behind your heel.
2. Lace the boot firmly, but not tight, and stand on the slope with your toes pointing down. Your toes must not touch the front. If they do, you will bruise your toenails on long descents.
3. Turn round and walk up the slope. Your heel may lift a little, but no more than a few millimetres.
4. Walk for at least ten minutes. A spot that presses in the shop gets worse on a trip, not better.

If your heel lifts too much, try the heel lock in the next section, or a slimmer model. If the boot is tight across the widest part of your foot, ask for the wide version of the Ridgeline Leather, or try a half size up.

Our boots come with a shaped footbed. If you wear insoles made for you by a doctor, bring them along, because they take up space inside the boot and can change the size you need. Swollen feet, thick socks and insoles all add up, so fit with everything you will really wear.

### Lacing for steep ground

The way you tie your laces changes how a boot fits. Our boots have open hooks at the top and small locking hooks where the ankle bends, so you can lace the lower and the upper part of the boot with different tension.

- **Heel lock**: lace normally up to the locking hooks, pull hard, and wrap the laces around each hook twice. This holds the heel down in the boot and stops your toes from hitting the front when you go downhill. It is the most useful trick for long descents.
- **Window lacing**: if the top of your foot hurts at one spot, skip one pair of hooks over that spot, so the lace does not press on it.
- **Loose top**: on long climbs, keep the upper hooks a little looser, so your ankle can bend forward. Tighten them again at the top before you go down.

Spare laces cost 6 a pair, in three lengths: 120 cm for low shoes, 150 cm for mid boots, and 180 cm for the Highpass and the Frostline. Round laces last longer than flat ones, but flat laces stay tied better. Our boots come with round laces, and the shops have flat ones too.

Check your laces before a long trip. A lace that is worn where it runs over a hook can break at the worst moment, so carry a spare one in your first aid kit.

### Breaking in new boots

New boots need time to soften and take the shape of your feet. The stiffer the boot, the longer it takes. Never take a new pair on a long trip straight from the box.

|---|---|

| Ridgeline Mid | About 30 km |
| Ridgeline Leather | About 60 km over two to three weeks |
| Highpass | About 50 km, with some hills |
| Frostline | About 20 km, in cold weather if you can |

Start by wearing the boots at home and on short walks to the shop. Then go on day walks that get longer each time, in the socks you will wear on the trip. Leather softens faster when it is warm, so wear leather boots indoors for a few evenings first.

Do not soak new boots in water to soften them, and do not put them on a radiator. Both can crack the leather and loosen the glue. If a spot rubs, put tape on that part of your foot before the next walk, and tell our shop staff. In the Lakeside and City centre shops we can stretch leather in one small area with a boot stretcher, for free.

If the boot still hurts after 50 km, it is not the right boot for your foot. We cannot take back boots that were worn outside, but our shop staff will help you find out what is wrong.

### Cleaning and drying

Mud dries out leather and blocks the pores of a membrane, so clean your boots after every walk, not just before the next one.

1. Take out the laces and the footbed.
2. Brush off dry mud with a stiff brush. For wet mud, rinse the boot under a cold tap and use a soft brush.
3. For stubborn dirt, use our Boot Cleaner gel. Never use washing-up liquid or laundry soap, because they leave a film that lets water in.
4. Once a season, rinse the inside with clean water to wash out the salt from your sweat. Salt makes leather hard.

Dry your boots slowly, at room temperature, with the footbed out. Fill them with newspaper and change it after an hour; it pulls out most of the water. Never dry boots on a radiator, next to a stove or a camp fire, or in the sun on a hot day. High heat makes leather shrink and crack, and it can melt the glue between the upper and the sole. Every year our workshop sees many boots with a sole that came loose this way.

Keep boots in a dry, airy place, not in a plastic bag or a hot car. If you will not wear them for a few months, clean them, treat the leather, and put a shoe tree or paper inside to keep the shape.

## Socks

Socks matter as much as boots. A good sock cushions the foot, moves sweat away from the skin and stops the boot from rubbing. Cotton holds sweat and stays wet, and wet skin blisters fast, so do not wear cotton on walks.

**Merino wool** is our first choice for most trips. It stays warm when damp, feels soft, and does not start to smell after one day. Pure merino wears through quickly, so our merino socks have nylon for strength and a little elastane so they stay in place.

**Synthetic** socks, made of polyester and nylon, dry faster than merino and last longer. They are good for hot weather and for walks through rivers, but they start to smell after a day.

**Liner socks** are thin socks you wear under a thicker sock. The two socks rub against each other instead of against your skin, which helps a lot if you get blisters easily.

Thickness matters too. Thin and light socks are cooler and suit low shoes. Medium socks suit most boots in spring, summer and autumn. Thick socks are for insulated boots and cold days. Always fit your boots with the sock you will wear, because a thick sock can take up half a size.

On a trip of several days, take two pairs and wear them in turns. In the evening, rinse the pair you wore in clean water and hang it on the outside of your backpack to dry while you walk the next day.

### Choosing the pole length

On flat ground, hold the pole by the grip with the tip on the floor next to your foot. Your elbow should bend at about 90 degrees, with your forearm level. A quick rule is your body height times 0.68.

| Your height | Pole length on flat ground |
|---|---|
| Under 155 cm | 100 cm |
| 155 to 165 cm | 110 cm |
| 166 to 175 cm | 115 cm |
| 176 to 185 cm | 125 cm |
| 186 to 195 cm | 130 cm |
| Over 195 cm | 135 cm |

Change the length when the ground changes. On a long climb, make the poles 5 to 10 cm shorter, so you do not lift your arms too high. Going down, make them 5 to 10 cm longer, so you can put them in front of you and take weight off your knees. On a path that runs across a slope, make the uphill pole shorter than the downhill one.

Put your hand up through the wrist strap from below, then hold the grip with the strap between your thumb and the grip. This way you can push down on the strap and keep a loose hand, which saves a lot of strength over a day.

On steep, rough ground where you could fall, take your hands out of the straps. If a pole gets stuck between rocks, it then cannot pull on your wrist. When you cross a stream on stones, set the poles a little longer than for flat ground and plant them firmly before each step.

## Water

Water from streams and lakes can look clean and still hold germs that make you ill, often a few days after the trip. When you do not know where the water comes from, treat it before you drink it, also high in the mountains.

**Filters** push water through very thin hollow fibres with holes of 0.1 micron. They remove bacteria, such as E. coli, and protozoa, such as giardia and cryptosporidium, and the water is ready at once. A filter does not remove viruses, which are too small to catch.

**Purifiers** also deal with viruses. Our purifier bottle has a second cartridge with holes of 0.02 micron that catches them, and purifying tablets kill them with chlorine dioxide. You need a purifier wherever human waste can get into the water: near villages, farms and huts, in countries where tap water is not safe, and after floods. In remote mountains in Europe, a filter is usually enough.

Neither a filter nor a tablet removes chemicals, salt or the taste of a muddy river. Let muddy water stand in a pot until the dirt sinks, then filter only the clear water from the top, or the filter clogs quickly.

Boiling also kills everything that can make you ill. Bring the water to a rolling boil for one minute; above 2,000 m, boil it for three minutes. It is safe, but it uses a lot of gas: about 10 g for every litre with the Spark stove. On a long trip, that adds up to an extra cartridge.

### Changing cartridges and looking after a filter

Hollow-fibre cartridges slowly fill up with dirt. Back-flush them after every trip and whenever the water comes through slowly: fill the cleaning syringe that comes with the filter with clean water and push it through the filter the wrong way. This pushes the dirt out of the fibres and brings back most of the speed.

Change the cartridge when one of these is true:

- It has cleaned the amount of water in the table. For a walker who drinks 3 litres a day, the Clearflow Squeeze lasts about 500 days of use.
- Back-flushing no longer brings back the speed.
- It has frozen with water inside. Ice cracks the fibres, and germs can then pass through without you seeing any change.
- For the Purestream Bottle, it is twelve months since you first used the cartridges, even if you have not reached 300 litres.

Freezing is the risk most people do not know about. Below 0 °C, put the filter in a plastic bag and sleep with it inside your sleeping bag, and carry it in an inside pocket by day. If you are not sure whether it froze, change the cartridge: it costs less than a week of being ill.

Write the date you started on a new cartridge on it with a marker. Before you store a filter for the winter, back-flush it, let it dry fully for three days with the caps off, and keep it somewhere dry. A damp filter in a closed bag can grow mould.

We do not put medicines such as painkillers in our kits, because the rules for selling them differ from country to country, and many people need their own. Add what you need yourself: your own medicines, painkillers, something for insect stings, and sun cream.

Check your kit before every season. Replace what you used on the last trip, because a kit that is missing the one thing you need is not much help. Plasters lose their stickiness after a few years, faster in a hot car, and sterile dressings have a date on the pack.

Our guides carry the Group kit on every guided trip, and it is the kit we advise if you lead a club walk.

### Blisters: prevent and treat

Most blisters come from heat, wet skin and rubbing, and you can stop most of them before they start.

Before the trip, break in your boots, cut your toenails short and straight, and walk in the socks you will take. If you know where you usually get blisters, put a strip of tape on that spot in the morning, before anything rubs.

On the trip, stop at the first warm spot. A hot spot is a blister that has not formed yet, and two minutes now save you days of pain. Take off the boot, let the skin dry, and put tape or a blister plaster on the spot. Change wet socks at lunch, and shake out stones and sand at once.

If a blister has formed, leave a small, closed blister alone and cover it with a blister plaster, which works like a second skin. Leave the plaster on until it comes off by itself. Only open a large blister that will burst anyway: use a clean needle at the edge, press the fluid out, and keep the loose skin on top as a cover. Then clean it and cover it, and put on a fresh dressing every day.

Watch for signs of infection: red skin that spreads, heat, pus or a fever. If you see them, go to a doctor.

Blister plasters stick best on clean, dry skin with no cream on it. Warm the plaster in your hand for a minute before you put it on, and round off the corners of tape so they do not catch on your sock.
